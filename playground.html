Ongoing research · Motion & interface

Playground

A running investigation into a single question: what does motion actually do for an interface? The answer these experiments keep landing on is that it decides whether a component reads as a set of parts being swapped, or as one living thing changing shape. Every component here is a working experiment. Press them.

Part II · The experiments

Every component is one living organism

A conventional interface is an assembly. Press it and a part is destroyed, another is created in its place, and the user pays — in attention — to work out what just happened. These are built the opposite way: as a single continuous body that never splits into a before and an after. It stretches, necks, floods and folds, but it is always the same mass. Motion is not decoration laid over the component — it is the thing keeping it alive.

The anatomy

One body, one mass. A core that limbs grow out of, membranes that stretch before they part, parts that stay attached while they travel. No state here is reached by replacing an element — every state is the same matter, rearranged.

The physics

Squash and stretch, surface tension, weight, follow-through — borrowed from character animation, where they exist for one reason: to make drawn matter read as alive rather than as geometry. A control that deforms under pressure is read as a body; one that cuts between states is read as a diagram.

The constraint

Strict monochrome, one shared token layer, no animation library. Removing colour was the most useful decision: with no accent to signal state, the form has to. An organism that only seems alive because it turned blue was never alive.

Part III · What it comes to

Where the body holds, and where it does not

Across all seven, the components that felt alive were the ones where no state change destroyed a part. That is the whole finding, and it is cheaper than it sounds: it is a sequencing decision far more often than it is an expensive effect. The radio only reads as living because its hole shuts before the old fill drains — same two properties, same duration, different order.

What held

Sequencing beats spectacle. Staggering two properties that already existed bought more life than any filter did — and the switch, the radio and the checkbox use no filter at all.

What it cost

The gooey merge is the expensive one. Its alpha-contrast pass is Chromium-only: Safari and Firefox render the blur and never re-sharpen it. A body that dissolves in two engines is a real price, not a footnote.

Where it fails

Not every change deserves a body. Motion earns its place only when removing it costs something — a lost position, an unnoticed change, an unclear cause. Everywhere else it is taxidermy: the shape of life on something inert.
